Capital spending versus physical build-out

Concrete and Compute

Big Tech is pouring money into physical infrastructure at a pace few industries have ever matched. But a capex figure lives in a spreadsheet, while a data center can be observed on the map, along with its grid connections and permit files. In this study, I track what can already be seen on the map at five flagship US campuses, using permits, regulatory filings, open construction data, satellite imagery and NASA night lights.

Attila Bécsi · personal analysis from public sources

Executive summary

  1. Only about a third of the flagship pipeline is switched on. Across five of the largest AI campuses, an estimated 3.4 GW of 10.5 GW is operating today. The rest is concrete, steel and paperwork, so much of this year's spending will only start earning its keep in 2027 and 2028.
  2. The money sits mostly in the shortest-lived asset. In Epoch AI's cost model, chips and servers make up 69% of capital at full build. The buildings you can see from orbit are the minority, which means AI investments depreciate far faster than the skyline suggests.
  3. Power now comes first. Louisiana's regulator approved new gas generation for Hyperion before construction started, and Colossus 2's turbines were permitted after they were reportedly already running. Anyone reading utility dockets sees the AI map months before it comes up on an earnings call.
  4. Night lights run about a year ahead of the megawatts. Across 36 US AI data-center sites, 75% brightened measurably before first power, against 22% of control points 8 km away (p = 0.0001), with a median lead of 12 months. Hyperion already glows about 50 times brighter than in 2023 with no power online. The signal confirms that a site is real and busy; it does not pin down the switch-on date any better than the land-clearing date does.
  5. Build speed varies more than tenfold. A converted factory went from construction start to first power in 4 months, while greenfield gigawatt sites took more than 3 years [B]. Schedules slip, and the permit files show by exactly how much.

A capex figure is not a data center

Capex guidance has become the most closely watched number in AI. It feeds the depreciation that will land on earnings, sets the revenue bar the investment has to clear, and increasingly shapes the credit markets that fund the build-out. Yet it is an aggregate. It says nothing about where the money goes, how much of it is concrete and how much is chips, or whether a campus announced last year has a grid connection yet.

That blind spot is growing. Financing is moving off balance sheet: Meta's Hyperion campus sits in a joint venture with funds managed by Blue Owl. The headline "investment" figures we see mix scopes, so a state incentive agreement may count buildings while a press release is about GPUs. And the real bottleneck has moved from capital to megawatts, which arrive on the timetables of utilities and regulators, not budget committees.

For anyone trying to see through the numbers, the good news is that physical infrastructure is hard to hide. Every campus needs land, zoning, a power approval, a substation and cooling. Each of those steps is either filed somewhere public or visible from space, and together they let us see details that no earnings call can offer.

Implications for capital allocators

Watch the power numbers, not the headlines

A dollar of announced capex starts earning only once the site is energized. Power approvals and interconnection dates therefore say more about when AI capacity will actually arrive than any investment headline.

Track the gap between permit and power

Permit targets and energization dates are both public, so we can measure the gap between them. For equity investors that gap is a revenue-timing input; for lenders it belongs in covenant and construction-risk analysis.

Separate the durable from the depreciating

With most capital sitting in hardware on five-to-six-year lives, the collateral that lenders and landlords can actually see and secure (the shell, the power, the land) is the smaller part of the cost. That changes how much of an AI campus is truly bankable.

Watch the regulatory record

Air permits, utility dockets and lawsuits surface operational and reputational risk long before it reaches an earnings call. Colossus 2's permit history shows how early those signals can appear.
